One chord progression will not unlock every pop song, but a well-chosen one gives your hands and
ears a useful reference. Learn G–D–Em–C cleanly and you will start recognising the same relationships
in other keys, tempos, and arrangements.

The progression is I–V–vi–IV. It is common enough to be worth learning and simple enough to practise
without turning the exercise into a theory lesson.

## Learn the four roles

Roman numerals name a chord by its position in the key. Uppercase numerals usually indicate major
chords and lowercase numerals minor chords. In G major, I–V–vi–IV looks like this:

```
I    V    vi   IV
G    D    Em   C
```

The same relationships move into another key without changing the numeral pattern:

```
Key    I     V     vi    IV
G      G     D     Em    C
C      C     G     Am    F
D      D     A     Bm    G
A      A     E     F#m   D
E      E     B     C#m   A
```

The [sample song](/songs/morning-light.md) uses `chords: G D Em C`, so you can inspect the file or
[see the chord sheet rendered](/#preview).

## Hear why the loop holds together

In a major key, I usually feels like home. V creates a strong pull towards it. vi shares notes with
I but changes the colour by moving to the relative minor. IV adds another stable major chord and can
set up the return.

Those tendencies make a flexible loop, but they do not make every melody fit. Rhythm, phrasing, and
which notes the melody stresses still matter. Think of I–V–vi–IV as a harmonic frame rather than a
formula that explains the entire song.

Starting the same set of chords on vi produces vi–IV–I–V. The chord vocabulary has not changed, but
the minor chord now sounds like the centre of the loop. Moving the starting point is a quick way to
hear how order changes the character of the same four chords.

## Practise without stopping

Use one bar per chord at a slow, even pulse:

1. Play G–D–Em–C four times without pausing between changes.
2. Keep the strumming simple enough that your fretting hand can arrive on time.
3. Hum a melody over the loop and notice which notes feel settled over each chord.
4. Move to C–G–Am–F and repeat the exercise.
5. Finish with Em–C–G–D so you hear the same chords from a different starting point.

If a transition breaks the pulse, isolate those two chords and alternate between them. Speed is not
the goal; an uninterrupted loop is.

When a song does not come with a reliable chart, use the same progression as one possible test—not
as a conclusion. [Finding the chords in a song](/blog/finding-the-chords-in-a-song) gives you a fuller
procedure for checking the tonic, bass, timing, and awkward bars.

## Move it to a singable key

A progression only becomes useful for singing when it sits comfortably in your range. Try the keys
in the table and listen for one where the high notes do not strain and the low notes still carry.

In chordlist, tapping the displayed progression opens the transpose control. You can step through
the twelve transpositions while you play; the change affects the current view and resets when you
leave the song, so the Markdown file stays as you wrote it. The
[playing guide](/docs#playing) covers transposition and autoscroll.

## Know what the exercise leaves out

Many songs use different loops, blues forms, borrowed chords, secondary dominants, or key changes.
Even a song built from these four chords may change their order or hold each one for a different
length. Learning I–V–vi–IV is therefore not a substitute for listening to the recording.

It is a reference point. Practise it in two keys today, then listen for one moment in another song
where the same movement appears. Recognition is the skill that makes four familiar chords useful.
